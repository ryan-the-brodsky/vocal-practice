# Mix Voice Exercises: How to Build a Connected Middle Register

Most pop, R&B, and musical-theater singing lives in the "mix." That is the part of your voice where chest and head overlap. Maybe your voice cracks somewhere in the E4–G4 range (tenors and baritones). Maybe it cracks in the G4–B4 range (mezzo-sopranos and sopranos). Maybe your high notes come out strained and shouty, or thin and disconnected. Either way, the middle of your voice is where the work needs to happen. Those note ranges are approximate. They vary by individual, training, and vowel. This article explains what mix voice is, what makes it break down, and which exercises build it.

---

## What is mix voice?

Mix voice also goes by middle voice, blended voice, or mixed registration. Two muscles set the balance. The thyroarytenoid (TA) shortens and thickens your vocal folds, which gives you chest voice. The cricothyroid (CT) stretches and thins them, which gives you head voice. In mix, neither muscle takes over completely. Both contribute.

Kochis-Jennings et al. (2014) measured this with electrodes on the muscles (EMG). It was a preliminary study of seven singers. Below 300 Hz, about D4, the TA dominated on chest notes and on head-mix and head notes. Above 300 Hz, every note showed CT-dominant or near-equal muscle activity, whatever the singers called it. They concluded that pitch, not the register label alone, mainly drove the CT:TA ratio above that point. That suggests the balance high in your range follows pitch more than a clean "chest vs. head" split implies. Mix looks more like a managed handover than a fixed, evenly split state.

Titze (2014) offered a second mechanism that sits alongside that one. In mix, he argued, the folds hold a nearly parallel, rectangular shape. That shape is "bi-stable." It can collapse suddenly into a more convergent shape (modal, or chest) or a divergent one (falsetto). On this view, mix is less about which muscle is firing. It is about holding one fold shape steady across changing pitches and air pressures. That is also why mix slips away when you push hard or let your breath support go.

Teaching methods name all of this differently. Speech Level Singing (Seth Riggs) talks about "connecting the registers" through a smooth bridge. Estill Voice Training calls mix a precise balance of thyroid tilt and TA activity. Complete Vocal Technique (Cathrine Sadolin) never uses the word "mix" at all. It splits the same territory into Neutral, Curbing, and Overdrive, three modes with different TA/CT ratios. The recipes differ and so do the explanations. Neither the Kochis-Jennings picture nor the Titze one lines up neatly with any single method's vocabulary. There is no one "correct" mix. Sounds run from chestier to headier, and both ends are useful.

## Why mix voice breaks down (and why that matters for CCM)

In pop, rock, R&B, and musical theater, the "chest-to-head break" gets treated as inevitable. It is not. It is a coordination failure, and it usually comes from one of these three things:

**Pulling chest weight too high.** The passaggio, or bridge, is the zone where your voice changes gears as pitch rises. When the TA stays dominant past the bridge, your folds cannot thin fast enough for the pitch. The result is a sharp, strained top note, or an abrupt crack into falsetto. In CCM, this is the most common mix breakdown.

**Flipping to disconnected falsetto.** This is the opposite problem. The TA lets go all at once and the CT takes over with no transition. The sound turns thin and breathy. It often shows up in singers who were told to "find head voice." Nobody taught them to carry chest energy up with them.

**Not enough CT training.** Head mix and head voice both need CT stretch. Singers who rarely leave chest voice often have an undertrained CT. Their voice then has no way to thin the folds up high. The only choices left are shout or disconnect. A computer model by Yin and Zhang (2013) tested this. CT activation was the main lever for raising the folds' natural vibration frequencies (eigenfrequencies). TA activation on its own cannot substitute for it in the upper range.

In every case, the fix is not to try harder in the trouble zone. It is to shift the TA/CT balance gradually before the bridge, then carry that new balance up.

## The role of consonants and onsets in mix exercises

Mix exercises lean on consonants like "ng," "b," and "g" for a reason. The way a note starts, its onset, changes how firmly your folds come together.

A "b" (as in "bub") is a bilabial stop. It briefly closes the airway at your lips. That builds a little pressure in your mouth before the vowel. When the pressure releases, your folds come together with enough closure for a clear onset. The start is not too pressed and not too breathy. That makes "bub" a useful onset for mix. It primes closure without the extra effort that "ha" (breathy) or a hard "g" (pressed) can produce.

The "ng" sound (as in "nay," "goog," or a held "ng") partly closes your throat and mouth at the back. Voice science calls that semi-occluded. Titze (2006) modeled semi-occluded vocal tract (SOVT) exercises on a computer. A partial narrowing at the lips or the soft palate makes the air above the folds push back harder (acoustic inertance). That back-pressure lowers the phonation threshold pressure, meaning the least air pressure under the folds needed to keep them vibrating. It also softens the collisions between the folds. That is why "ng" and lip trills tend to feel easier on high notes than open vowels do.

Two caveats belong with that. The 2006 paper ran a computer simulation of a self-oscillating model, not direct measurements of healthy singers during training. Later studies offer some empirical support in clinical groups, but there is little direct randomized-trial evidence that SOVT work develops healthy singing voices. Most teachers across Estill, CVT, and SLS still cite "ng" sirens as effective for smoothing the chest-to-head shift, based on applied teaching experience.

## Mix voice exercises to try

These are ordered from easiest to most demanding. Each one works the bridge and asks your voice to carry a coordination from one side of it to the other.

### 1. "Ng" siren

Make a steady "ng" sound, the consonant at the end of "sing." Glide slowly from your lowest comfortable pitch up to your highest, then back down. The partly closed position limits how hard you can press. Aim for a smooth slide. Any crack or sudden jump marks a boundary where the balance shifted all at once.

Many teachers across Estill, SLS, and CVT use "ng" sirens for exactly that reason. The position makes it harder to over-press at the top and harder to go breathy at the bottom. The evidence here comes from teaching practice more than from experiments. Still, the agreement across methods is notable.

### 2. "Nay" on a five-tone scale

Sing "nay" on a 1-2-3-2-1 scale pattern. Make it bratty, witchy, slightly annoying. The narrow, bright /æ/ vowel keeps the CT engaged. The nasal twang narrows the space just above your folds, which adds ring without extra breath pressure. That is useful for CCM mix. Brett Manning's Singing Success program links "nay" arpeggio drills to fold closure in a lighter coordination. In plain terms, the mix carries without getting heavy. Again, this is agreement among experienced teachers, not a controlled trial on "nay" itself.

### 3. "Oo" on an octave scale

Sing "oo" as in "who" on a 1-8-1 pattern, up an octave and back. The rounded vowel naturally centers the larynx and encourages CT activation. Many singers find "oo" self-balancing. Bright vowels make it easy to over-press, while "oo" puts a natural ceiling on pressure. Pick a comfortable tempo. You are after smoothness through the octave jump, not speed.

### 4. Descending arpeggio from the octave: Bub Mix Voice

This is the exercise in the practice widget on this page. Start on the octave above the tonic, scale degree 8. Come down through the 5th and the 3rd to the tonic: 8–5–3–1. Sing "bub" on every note.

Starting high has a purpose. Head-mix coordination is usually easier to find when your voice enters from above instead of climbing up from chest. Once you are in that lighter coordination, the descending arpeggio carries it down through the bridge into chest range. That is the reverse of the "pulling chest up" failure. The keys climb by half steps from the bottom of your range, up through the bridge, then back down. So the exercise works the mix boundary in both directions.

Quarter notes at 100 bpm give each note about 0.6 seconds. That is long enough to hear whether the tone stays connected, and short enough that the transition does not feel labored. Keep the "bub" onset relaxed. Let your lips release with the airflow instead of popping the consonant hard.

## Common mistakes in mix voice training

**Trying to mix too loud too soon.** Mix is a balance, not a volume. Practice at a moderate dynamic (mf) so you can hear whether the tone stays even. At full volume, what you feel in your body stops being a reliable guide. You feel effort, not balance.

**Staying in the comfortable range.** Mix only develops when you work near the bridge. If every exercise sits well below or well above that zone, the balance never changes.

**Confusing mix with falsetto.** In falsetto, which some CCM singers call head voice, the folds barely come together. The tone is airy or very light. Mix has more fold contact and more chest resonance. If the tone thins out to nothing on high notes, you are in falsetto, not mix. A light "nay" onset usually brings the closure back.

**Expecting fast results.** TA/CT coordination responds to repeated, consistent training. Most singers notice steadier registration over weeks of daily practice, not inside a single session.

## Frequently asked questions

**What does mix voice sound like?**
Mix sounds fuller than pure head voice and less strained than pulled chest. In pop and R&B, most singers use a chestier mix low in a chorus and a headier mix at the top. Stevie Wonder, Ariana Grande, and John Legend are often described as having a strong mix. Their exact balance on any given note still varies, though. It is hard to confirm without laryngeal imaging, meaning a camera look at the folds.

**Is mix voice the same as falsetto?**
No. Falsetto in classical usage, and what many CCM teachers call "head voice," involves minimal TA activity. The folds also often sit slightly apart. Mix keeps a meaningful TA contribution and more fold closure, which gives it more carrying power. In Titze's bi-stable model, mix is that nearly parallel fold shape, and it can hold itself across a range of pitches. Falsetto is a divergent fold posture instead. Some methods use "falsetto" and "head voice" to mean the same thing, and others separate them. The practical test is carrying power. Mix usually projects clearly in a mid-sized room without amplification, and falsetto often does not.

**Can men develop mix voice?**
Yes. In classical convention, a tenor's transition zone (zona di passaggio) runs roughly D4–G4, between the primo and secondo passaggio. Baritones sit a step or two lower. CCM singers often break at slightly different spots, depending on vowel, dynamic, and training. "Ng" sirens, "oo" scales, and descending arpeggios that start above the bridge all apply to male voices. The Bub Mix Voice exercise includes tenor and baritone voice parts.

**How long does it take to develop mix voice?**
There is no well-controlled study on this timeline. Teaching experience suggests most singers start to feel a difference within several weeks of regular practice (10–15 minutes daily). A mix you can rely on under performance conditions takes longer, often a few months. Individual variation is substantial.

**What is the passaggio?**
Passaggio is Italian for "passage." It is the zone where the balance between chest and head shifts as pitch rises. Most voices have two passaggi, a lower one (primo passaggio) and an upper one (secondo passaggio). In classical convention, a tenor's primo falls roughly around D4–E4 and the secondo around F#4–G4. Baritones sit a step or two lower. For female voices, the soprano secondo is typically around A4–B♭4. Mezzo-sopranos sit a whole step lower, at approximately G4–A♭4. CCM singers often land in spots that differ from those classical estimates, and the zone can shift with conditioning over time. None of this is a flaw. Every voice has a passaggio, and mix training is largely the art of crossing it without an audible bump.

**Should I see a teacher to learn mix voice?**
A teacher who can watch your coordination in real time will get you further than practicing alone, especially if you have a persistent break or strain. Hoarseness is a separate question. If your voice is persistently hoarse (dysphonia), not just tired from practice, the AAO-HNS 2018 clinical guideline recommends evaluation by a laryngologist when it does not resolve or improve within **four weeks**. Two weeks is sometimes cited informally, but four weeks is the current guidance.
