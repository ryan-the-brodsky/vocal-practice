# How to Practice Singing

Most singers practice in a way that feels productive but isn't. They run through songs, hit the hard part, back up, and run through them again. This article covers why that approach limits improvement, and what to do instead. It draws on voice science and on how working teachers train contemporary commercial music (CCM): pop, rock, R&B, and musical theater.

## Why Most Singing Practice Stalls

K. Anders Ericsson's research on expert performance found that **not all practice is equal**. His 1993 study of violinists and pianists is still widely cited, though its "10,000 hours" oversimplification became notorious. What set the top performers apart was *deliberate* practice: work that is (1) targeted at a specific skill gap, (2) at the edge of current ability, and (3) paired with immediate, accurate feedback.

Running through a song for the third time in a row fails all three tests. You are repeating things you can already do. You are coasting on your memory of the song instead of training the technique underneath it. And your feedback, meaning how the singing feels, is unreliable: the sound you hear inside your head is heavily filtered by bone conduction and rarely matches what a listener hears.

This doesn't mean dropping songs from your practice. It means most of your improvement time should come from **exercises that target one specific vocal coordination**, not from song run-throughs.

## How Long Should You Practice?

There is no single right answer, but the research offers some useful constraints.

Ericsson found that the best musicians practiced in focused blocks of no more than about 90 minutes before taking a break. Benefits also declined significantly above roughly **four hours per day** of deliberate practice. Across concentration-heavy fields more broadly, focused work tends to hold its quality for **one to two hours**. That last figure is a rough pattern across studies, not a precise prescription from any single experiment.

The cap matters more for singing than for most instruments. Your vocal folds, the two small bands of tissue in your throat that vibrate to make sound, are mucous membrane rather than muscle. Overuse makes them swell, and that swelling degrades the very coordination you are trying to train.

Here is a more practical model for singers at most levels: **15 to 30 minutes of focused vocal exercise, four to five days per week**, plus song work. Consistent short sessions accumulate more learning than occasional marathon sessions. The research on distributed, or "spaced," practice is consistent on this point across many motor and mental skills. Voice-specific trial evidence is thin, but the general principle is well supported and widely applied by teachers.

One caveat: voice science has no clean randomized trial on daily singing duration for CCM singers specifically. These numbers are informed estimates from experienced teachers rather than controlled study results.

## The Structure of a Useful Practice Session

A well-organized session has three phases. Each one does a different kind of physical work.

### Phase 1: Warm-up (5–10 minutes)

Before you ask anything hard of your voice, the goal is to make sound easier to start. The technical name for that is lowering your phonation threshold: the minimum air pressure and muscular effort needed to keep the folds vibrating. Research supports **semi-occluded vocal tract exercises**, usually shortened to SOVTs, as the most efficient warm-up tool.

Semi-occluded just means partly closed at the front of your mouth. Lip trills, tongue trills, humming, singing through a straw, and "ng" hums all count. Ingo Titze's 2006 paper in the *Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research* laid out the physical rationale. A partial closure at the lips or teeth raises the air pressure just above the folds. That lets them vibrate with **less mechanical stress** and at **lower effort levels**.

A 2021 single-subject case study by Castillo-Allendes, Cantor-Cutiva, and Hunter used a one-minute warm-up of humming through a straw into water. It found measurable acoustic improvements, including decreased jitter and shimmer and an improved harmonics-to-noise ratio. Those improvements persisted over seven weeks of daily measurement. Because this was an n=1 study, read it as preliminary evidence that such warm-ups produce real acoustic changes in individual voice users, not as a large-scale population finding.

The practical recipe is two to three minutes of lip trills or "ng" hums, moving up and back down through your comfortable range. Keep everything light. Save your louder, more driven sounds for after the voice is warm.

### Phase 2: Targeted technique work (10–15 minutes)

This is the deliberate-practice core. Pick **one or two specific coordination problems** to work on, not "get better in general." Here are three common ones.

- **Breath support:** If your long notes sag flat, or your tone wobbles at the end of a phrase, your ribcage is probably collapsing. Classical teachers call the fix *appoggio*, Italian for "lean." You keep the lower ribs expanded as if you were still inhaling, even while you exhale and sing. That holds up the air pressure below the folds through the whole phrase. Many CCM coaches teach the same thing without the Italian: "keep the ribs buoyant," "don't let the chest drop."

- **Cord closure:** If your tone is breathy, or your notes come in late and wobbly, your folds are not coming together firmly enough. Onset exercises help. Start a note from vocal fry, or put a consonant like "m," "n," or "ng" in front of the vowel. Both cue the folds to close before the airflow begins.

- **The passaggio:** Italian for "passage." This is the transition zone between chest voice (lower notes, fuller sound, driven mainly by the thyroarytenoid muscle) and head voice (higher notes, lighter quality, driven mainly by the cricothyroid). In CCM it typically falls around E4–F#4 for male voices and A4–B4 for female voices. Those figures come from teaching experience and acoustic research, individual variation is wide, and CCM voice-type labels are approximate rather than fixed categories. Most untrained singers either break audibly in this zone or avoid it by staying in one voice. Crossing it smoothly is a central goal of training.

The methods disagree about how to cross it. Classical teaching and the Speech Level Singing (SLS) lineage tend to emphasize vowel modification as you ascend, narrowing /iː/ toward /ɪ/ and /ɑː/ toward /ʌ/. SLS came from Seth Riggs and aims at seamless connection between chest and head voice without lifting the voice box. Complete Vocal Technique (CVT), Cathrine Sadolin's system, organizes production by vocal mode rather than by register. Somatic Voicework is sensation-based and plays down anatomical vocabulary in favor of what the singer feels. Both of those put more weight on blending the two voices through resonance quality. Estill Voice Training teaches specific voice-box configurations as separable figures, such as thyroid tilt and aryepiglottic narrowing, then builds voice qualities from combinations of them. All of these are real approaches with their own evidence bases, not competing superstitions. They are different points of attack on the same coordination challenge. If one doesn't click, try another.

**One technique goal per session** is a useful discipline. More than that and attention fragments, feedback gets murky, and the session devolves into the same unfocused repetition you started with.

### Phase 3: Application (5–10 minutes)

End with repertoire, but carry one specific intention over from Phase 2. If you worked on breath support, apply it in one difficult phrase. If you worked on the passaggio, pick a chorus that crosses it. This transfers the isolated coordination into context without abandoning everything you just built.

## The Role of Recording Yourself

Your own impression of your singing is consistently inaccurate. That is not a bad ear. Bone conduction is the reason. Vibration travelling through your skull adds significant coloration to what you hear from the inside. What everyone else hears reaches them through the air alone.

Recording yourself, even with a phone voice memo, gives you access to that external signal. Play it back immediately after a take. The gap between what you thought you sang and what you hear on playback is useful diagnostic data, not cause for shame. Research on self-voice perception confirms the discrepancy and finds it consistent. Bone conduction is part of a multi-modal experience of your own voice that recordings strip away.

Recording is consistent across methods, and it helps at every level. What matters is reviewing the recording with a specific question in mind, such as "Did the phrase end flat?" or "Did the vowel shift between chest and head?" Listening generically for "how it sounds" teaches you far less.

## Where Methods Legitimately Disagree

This matters, because it affects how you use online resources and who you study with.

**Vowel modification:** Classical teaching and much of the SLS lineage hold that vowels should narrow as pitch ascends, /æ/ toward /ʌ/ and /i/ toward /ɪ/. Some CCM belt-focused approaches prefer the opposite up high, keeping vowels brighter and more open. Neither position is wrong. They aim at different sounds and shape resonance differently.

**Voice-box height (larynx):** Classical training often explicitly trains a low larynx. In CCM the more common goal is a *neutral* larynx, neither deliberately lowered nor pulled up by tension. Singers importing an operatic sound into pop often carry a low voice box that muddies their upper range.

**How much anatomy to teach:** Estill Voice Training and CVT come with a lot of biomechanical vocabulary, such as thyroid tilt and aryepiglottic narrowing. SLS and some Somatic Voicework approaches skip the anatomy and work from sensation cues and acoustic targets. Both can produce results. The question is what kind of learner you are.

Knowing that the methods disagree, and why, lets you weigh the advice you run into instead of taking any one coach's approach as the only possible truth.

## Try It: Five-Note Scale (Mee May Mah)

The **Five-Note Scale (Mee May Mah)** is a standard entry point in CCM voice teaching. It steps up and back down across scale degrees 1–2–3–4–5–4–3–2–1 on the Italian vowel series: *mee–may–mah–moh–moo–moh–mah–may–mee*. The exercise runs at 88 bpm in eighth notes, with the piano doubling the melody as a reference.

**What it trains:** This one is filed under *resonance*. The goal is a consistent tonal core that doesn't shift dramatically from vowel to vowel. The "m" at the start of each syllable closes the cords before the vowel opens, which gives a cleaner, more centered tone than starting straight on a vowel. Rotating through five vowels in one phrase trains you to hold that consistency while your tongue and lips keep moving.

**What to listen for:** Does the "mah" at the top of the scale feel and sound as full as the "mee" at the bottom? Does the "moh" drift flat or breathy? Does the phrase shape stay even on the way back down? Any vowel that consistently drops out is a specific coordination gap to bring into Phase 2 of your next session.

The exercise moves up a half step each time through, so it covers your range systematically. Vocal Habit scores your pitch accuracy after each key.

## FAQ

### How often should I practice singing?

Consistent short sessions beat occasional long ones. For most adult learners, four to five focused sessions per week of 20–30 minutes each build skill more efficiently than one or two long sessions. The voice responds to regular stimulus followed by rest. The tissue in your folds needs recovery time, not just accumulated volume.

### Is it bad to practice singing every day?

It depends on how you practice. Light warm-ups and moderate technique work every day are generally fine, and they build the habit. The risk is loud, high-effort singing every day with no rest days. Belt rehearsals, sustained high notes, and heavy chest voice create cumulative fatigue. Build in at least one or two lighter days per week, and listen to your voice. A voice that sounds rough or feels effortful at the start of a session likely needs recovery, not more pushing.

### Can I practice singing without a teacher?

Yes, with some caveats. Some exercises have constraints built in, including SOVTs and patterns that step through the passaggio in small intervals. They teach coordination without a teacher in the room, because they regulate air pressure and the chest-to-head transition on their own to some extent. What is harder alone is diagnosis. When something isn't working, a single session with an experienced teacher can often name a pattern a singer has missed for months of self-study.

### What should I warm up my voice with?

Start with semi-occluded exercises: lip trills, an "ng" hum, or humming up and down a scale. They make sound easier to start and warm the folds with less mechanical impact than open-vowel singing. Two to five minutes is usually enough. Save louder, more driven sounds for after.

### Why do I sound worse on recordings than in my head?

Bone conduction, the vibration travelling through your skull, adds coloration to the sound you hear internally. Outside listeners and recordings only get the air-conducted signal, which sounds different. This gap is normal and not a sign of a bad voice. Recording yourself regularly and listening critically is one of the most efficient ways to close it.

### Is it normal to sound worse some days than others?

Yes. The tissue in your vocal folds responds to hydration, sleep quality, allergies, reflux, hormone changes, and how much you spoke or sang the day before. Day-to-day variation is not a meaningful measure of whether you're improving. The trend over weeks and months is the right unit of measurement.

### When should I see a doctor about my voice?

If hoarseness or a significant change in your voice lasts **four weeks or more**, see a laryngologist, an ENT specialist who can scope the vocal folds. That is what the 2018 AAO-HNS Clinical Practice Guideline recommends, rather than waiting it out or working around the symptom. Go sooner if you have throat pain, difficulty swallowing, a neck mass, or a history of tobacco use.
