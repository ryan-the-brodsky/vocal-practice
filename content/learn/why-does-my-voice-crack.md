# Why Does My Voice Crack When I Sing?

You're climbing toward a high note. It's going fine. Then the sound splits, drops out, or flips into something thin and squeaky. It's one of the most discouraging things that can happen when you sing, especially in front of other people. If it keeps happening, two questions probably come first. Let's answer them right away.

**Is this normal?** Yes. Almost every singer cracks, including trained ones warming up cold. It is one of the most common things beginners ask about. It is a coordination hiccup, not a sign you "can't sing."

**Am I damaging my voice?** Almost certainly not. An occasional crack while you sing or practice is your voice fumbling a gear change. It is not an injury. It does not mean you tore or hurt anything. There is one exception to watch. *Persistent* hoarseness that lasts for weeks is a different situation, and it's covered near the end.

Here's what is happening, and what to do about it.

---

## What a voice crack is: your voice shifts gears

Think of your voice like a car with two gears. There's a lower, heavier gear you use for speaking and for lower singing. There's a lighter, higher gear for higher notes. As you sing up in pitch, at some point you have to shift from the heavy gear into the light one.

A voice crack is what happens when that shift is done **abruptly instead of smoothly**. It's like popping a manual transmission without using the clutch. The sound lurches, drops out for an instant, or flips suddenly into something lighter and airier.

Here's the plain version of the mechanism first. The technical words come after, in case you meet them elsewhere.

- Your vocal folds are the two small bands in your throat that vibrate to make sound. Muscles can set them **short and thick**, for lower and heavier notes, or **long and thin**, for higher ones.
- To sing higher, the folds have to get thinner and longer. Hang on to the thick, heavy setting too long as you climb, and eventually it can't hold the pitch. The voice then snaps into the thin setting all at once. That snap is the crack.

Now the technical words. The "heavy gear" is **chest voice**, driven mostly by the **thyroarytenoid (TA) muscle**, which shortens and thickens the folds. The "light gear" is **head voice**, driven by the **cricothyroid (CT) muscle**, which stretches and thins them. The zone where the handoff happens is called the **passaggio**. That's Italian for "passage," and it means the crossover area between the two gears. The smooth, blended coordination that carries you across it is called **mix voice**. A crack is a failed, sudden handoff instead of a gradual blend.

This isn't just a metaphor. Researchers have measured those two muscles directly with EMG. Kochis-Jennings et al. (2014), a small and explicitly preliminary study of seven singers, found that above roughly 300 Hz (around D4) the balance shifts toward CT-dominant or near-equal muscle activity. That held no matter which gear the singer said they were using. The handoff is measurable, not imagined. Titze (2014) modeled *why* it can happen so abruptly. Near the crossover, the folds sit in a "bi-stable" shape that can collapse suddenly into either the heavier or the lighter setting. That is exactly the knife's-edge feeling of a note about to crack.

---

## Why it happens (the usual causes)

Cracks almost always trace back to one of a few things. Most singers have a favorite.

**Carrying too much "chest weight" up too high.** This is the most common cause on the way up to a high note. You keep the heavy setting engaged past the point where it can hold the pitch. The folds can't thin fast enough. The note either strains and then snaps, or flips abruptly into falsetto. Computational modeling by Yin and Zhang (2013) shows why you can't just muscle through it. CT stretch is the main lever for reaching higher pitches, and TA effort alone can't stand in for it up top. Pushing harder in the heavy gear is the opposite of what the note needs.

**Flipping straight into disconnected falsetto.** This is the mirror image. The heavy setting lets go all at once, and the light setting takes over with no blend. The tone suddenly goes thin and breathy. It's common in singers who were told to "just go into head voice" without learning to carry any strength across the bridge.

**Tension.** A clenched jaw, a gripping tongue root, or a voice box (larynx) that jams upward to "reach" for a note all interfere with the fine muscular balance the folds need. Squeeze in the wrong place and the gear change can't happen cleanly.

**Breath support that collapses.** Pitch depends on steady air pressure below the folds. Sundberg's foundational acoustic work on singing showed that this pressure under the folds (subglottal pressure) has to be nudged steadily *upward* as pitch rises. If your ribs cave in and that support sags right as you reach the tricky zone, the note loses its foundation. It is then far more likely to break.

**A cold or tired voice.** Cracks are far more common on a voice that hasn't warmed up. The crossover zone is usually the first thing to wobble when you're fatigued or your folds are dry (more on dryness below). This is why a crack in the first minute of singing often disappears after a proper warm-up.

Notice what these have in common. None of them is a talent problem. They are coordination or condition problems, and coordination and condition are trainable.

---

## Is it normal? (Two different "normal"s)

People mean two different things when they ask whether cracking is normal. Each one deserves its own answer.

**Everyday singing cracks: normal.** Your voice may break on high notes, at the gear change, or when you sing cold. That is ordinary, and nearly everyone learning to navigate their range does it. It reflects a coordination that isn't yet automatic, and it reliably decreases as that coordination develops with practice.

**Puberty voice change: also normal, and temporary.** Maybe you're an adolescent, or the parent of one, and the voice is cracking, dropping, and flipping unpredictably day to day. That's a distinct, developmental process. During male puberty the voice box grows, the vocal folds lengthen substantially, and the speaking pitch drops by roughly an octave. According to Voice Science Works, that descent runs at roughly a semitone per month, over a transition that typically lasts about 18 months. It usually begins around age 13 on average, within a normal range spanning roughly 10.5 to 16.5 years. During that window the old higher voice and the emerging lower one don't yet have a stable anchor, so the voice flips between them. It is "not a malfunction," as they put it, but "a system in transition." The reassuring part: guided singing that stays in a comfortable range is considered safe and beneficial through voice change. You don't have to stop singing while it settles. Voice change is most dramatic in boys. Girls' voices also change at puberty, but far more subtly: the folds lengthen much less, and the speaking pitch typically drops only a few semitones.

## Is it harmful? (Usually not, plus the one flag to watch)

An occasional crack is not damage. It's a coordination event, not a tearing or bruising of tissue, and singers crack all the time without harm. You don't need to panic when it happens, and you don't need to stop singing over it.

Two sensible boundaries, though:

- **Don't force through pain.** Singing can feel effortful, but it should not hurt. Sharp pain, a burning throat, or a note you can only reach by straining hard is a signal. Back off instead of pushing harder.
- **Watch for *persistent* hoarseness.** A crack is momentary. A rough, raspy, or persistently weak voice that hangs around for weeks is different. The AAO-HNS 2018 clinical practice guideline recommends seeing a laryngologist (an ENT who specializes in the voice) if hoarseness (dysphonia) does not resolve or improve within **four weeks**. Go sooner if you also have a neck mass, difficulty breathing, coughing up blood, or a history of tobacco use. That's the four-week rule. The older folk advice of "two weeks" isn't what current guidance says.

## How to stop it: the fixes that work

The instinct after a crack is to try harder next time. That usually makes it worse. Most cracks come from *too much* of something, usually too much weight, too much air, or too much squeeze. The fixes below all work by lowering the effort and letting the gear change happen gradually.

**1. Lip trills (bubble lips), the classic crack fix.** Blow air through loosely closed lips so they flutter ("brrr"). Glide gently up through the spot where you'd normally crack, then back down. Teachers call this a **semi-occluded vocal tract (SOVT)** exercise. That's a fancy way of saying you partly close off the front of your mouth, which changes the air pressure in a way that helps the folds. Titze (2006) worked out the physics. Partly closing the lips lowers the **phonation threshold pressure**, the minimum air pressure needed to keep the folds vibrating, and reduces the impact forces between the folds. In plain terms, lip trills make it *easier* to keep the sound going across the gear change. They also make it very hard to over-push, so the voice tends to find the smooth handoff on its own. There is a caveat. Titze's 2006 rationale comes from a computer simulation of a self-oscillating vocal model, not direct measurements of singers. Later clinical studies add support, but strong controlled evidence in healthy singers specifically is still limited. The mechanism is well-regarded across teaching methods. The lip-trill exercise embedded below walks up and down through your whole crossover zone for exactly this reason.

**2. "Ng" sirens.** Make the "ng" sound at the end of "sing." Slide slowly from your lowest comfortable pitch up to your highest and back down, like a slow siren. The "ng" partly closes off the sound the same way a lip trill does, so it limits how hard you can press. Keep the slide smooth. Wherever you hear a sudden jump or crack is exactly the spot where your gear change is abrupt, and slow, light sirens are how you teach it to smooth out.

**3. Blend instead of switch (build your mix).** The long-term fix is developing that middle coordination where the two gears overlap instead of clunking between them. That's the whole point of mix voice, and it's a skill you build over weeks, not a trick you flip on. See our guide to [mix voice exercises](/learn/mix-voice-exercises) for the full progression. For an easy, connected top end, which is the raw material a good mix is made from, see [head voice exercises](/learn/head-voice-exercises).

**4. Support the note.** Keep your ribcage comfortably open, as if you were still in the middle of an easy inhale, rather than letting it collapse the moment you start the phrase. Steady support keeps the air pressure under the note stable through the gear change, which is when it's most likely to sag and break. This isn't about pushing *more* air. It's about keeping the flow even.

**5. Release tension and warm up first.** Loosen the jaw, unclench the tongue, and don't ask a cold voice to leap to your highest notes. Two to three minutes of lip trills or gentle sirens first lowers the effort it takes to cross the bridge. Cracks that show up cold often just vanish once you're warm.

**6. Stay hydrated (a modest factor, not a magic fix).** Dry folds are stiffer and take more pressure to get vibrating, which makes cracking more likely, especially up high. A review by Sivasankar and Leydon (2010) found converging evidence that dehydration increases the stiffness and viscosity of vocal-fold tissue and raises the effort needed to produce voice. The same review is careful about the limits, though. It notes that robust proof that *drinking more* reliably improves voice outcomes is still limited. Treat hydration as sensible vocal hygiene that reduces one risk factor, not a cure. If you tend to breathe through your mouth (which dries the folds) or sing in dry air, it matters a bit more.

## A simple practice path

If you want a concrete order to work in:

1. **Warm up with lip trills or humming through a straw** for 2–3 minutes. Glide gently up and down through the note where you usually crack. Keep the effort low.
2. **Do slow "ng" sirens** across your full range, keeping the slide smooth and quiet. Notice where the wobble is. That's your target zone.
3. **Work a mix exercise** near that crossover zone, not far below or above it, at a moderate volume. Mix develops by spending time *in* the bridge, not around it.
4. **Keep sessions short and frequent.** Ten to fifteen minutes most days beats one long weekly grind. Motor coordination consolidates through repetition over time. Short sessions also keep you from tiring out, and a tired voice cracks more.

Expect gradual progress. Crossing between the gears is a motor skill. With consistent work, the crack tends to get less disruptive over a few weeks, and reliably smooth over months. It won't happen in a single session.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why does my voice crack only on high notes?**
Because high notes are where the gear change happens. Lower notes stay comfortably in your heavier gear, so there's no handoff to fumble. As you climb toward the passaggio, the folds have to shift to a thinner, lighter setting. If that shift is abrupt, the note cracks right at the crossover. It is usually abrupt because you carried too much weight up, or because the voice is cold or tired. Practicing gentle lip trills and sirens through that specific zone is the direct fix.

**Is it bad to keep singing if my voice keeps cracking?**
No. An occasional crack isn't harmful, and you learn to cross the gear change smoothly by practicing *through* it, not by avoiding it. The one exception is pain. If singing hurts, or your voice is persistently hoarse for weeks (not just tired for an afternoon), ease off. If hoarseness lasts four weeks or more, see a laryngologist.

**Will my voice stop cracking as I get better?**
For most singers, yes. Cracking is a coordination issue, and coordination improves with consistent practice. It won't necessarily vanish overnight, and even trained singers can crack on a cold voice. But cracks get rarer and milder as your mix develops and you learn to manage weight, breath, and tension through the crossover.

**Why does my voice crack when I'm not even singing high?**
A few possibilities. If you're an adolescent, ongoing puberty voice change makes the voice flip unpredictably across its whole range for a while. That's normal and temporary. Otherwise, cracks lower in the voice often come from a dry or fatigued voice, from folds that meet too loosely (not enough contact, so the tone destabilizes), or from a sudden gear change you're making without realizing it. Warming up, hydrating, and starting your notes more firmly with a gentle "ng" or "nay" usually helps.

**How do I fix voice cracks fast?**
There's no instant cure. The fastest relief is almost always to warm up properly, drop the volume, and do lip trills or "ng" sirens through the trouble spot at low effort. Many cracks are just a cold or over-pushed voice, and both of those respond within minutes. The *lasting* fix is building a mix so the crossover is smooth by default, and that takes weeks of regular practice.

**Is voice cracking during puberty something to worry about?**
Generally no. It's a normal developmental stage. The voice box is growing and the voice is dropping about an octave over roughly a year and a half, so it flips between the old and new settings while it lands. Guided singing that stays in a comfortable range through that period is considered safe. Do mention it to a doctor if an adolescent's voice change hasn't started at all by around 15–16, or if hoarseness persists well beyond the transition. Everyday cracking during the change itself is expected.

---

**Medical note:** Occasional voice cracks while singing are not a sign of injury. But if you have hoarseness or a voice change that does not improve within **four weeks**, see a laryngologist (an ENT who specializes in the voice). The AAO-HNS 2018 clinical practice guideline recommends evaluation at that point, or sooner if you also have symptoms such as a neck mass, difficulty breathing, coughing up blood, or a history of tobacco use. Singing should feel effortful at times but never painful. If it hurts, stop.
