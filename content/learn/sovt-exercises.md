# Semi-Occluded Vocal Tract Exercises: What They Are and How to Use Them

You have almost certainly done a semi-occluded vocal tract exercise, or SOVT, without knowing it had a name. Lip trills, humming, tongue trills, and singing through a narrow straw all count. Voice clinics and singing teachers recommend them more than almost any other warm-up. The physics behind them is simple, and it explains what the exercises are doing to your voice.

## What does "semi-occluded vocal tract" mean?

"Semi-occluded" means partly closed. The tract is your throat and mouth: the tube your sound travels through on the way out. When you lip-trill or sing through a narrow straw, the opening at your lips gets small but never seals shut. That small opening creates back-pressure, a gentle push of air that bounces back down toward your vocal folds. The folds feel that push, and it changes the way they vibrate.

Voice scientist Ingo Titze, at the National Center for Voice and Speech (University of Utah), proposed the mechanism. Partly closing the airway raises **acoustic inertance**, which means the column of air in front of the folds gets harder to shove into motion. That heavier air column does something useful. It lowers the smallest amount of lung pressure you need to start the folds vibrating and keep them going. Researchers call that number the **phonation threshold pressure (PTP)**. Titze set out the theory in a 2006 peer-reviewed paper in the *Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research* (Titze, 2006).

A lower threshold means the folds can vibrate with less effort. Less effort means they collide with less force, so they may warm up without stress.

There is a catch. Inertance pushes the threshold down, but the straw's resistance to airflow pushes it back up, and the two partly cancel out. The net benefit for a healthy singer holds up. It is also more modest than early writing sometimes implied.

That is why Titze put lip trills, tongue trills, humming, and straw phonation first in his "Five Best Vocal Warm-Up Exercises" in the *Journal of Singing* (2001).

## What the research shows

There is research behind these exercises, and it is still developing. Here is a fair summary.

**Less tension in the muscles around the voice box.** A 2021 study (Savareh et al., *Journal of Voice*) followed 11 male singers through a 20-minute session of lip trills, humming, and straw phonation. The researchers took surface electromyography readings, which measure muscle activity through the skin, plus several sound measurements. Afterward, the outer muscles that tense and lift the larynx under effort were quieter. Two sound measures, fundamental frequency and first formant, also moved in the direction you see when singing takes less effort. Only 11 singers took part, so the finding may not generalize.

**Airflow and fold contact both change, but by different amounts in different people.** Dargin and Searl (2015, *Journal of Voice*, 29(2):155–164) measured airflow, sound pressure, and vocal fold contact in four singers across three SOVTs. Average airflow and contact quotient, the share of each vibration cycle the folds spend touching, both tended to rise. How much they rose varied a lot from singer to singer. The effect points the same way in everyone; how big it gets is personal. With only four participants, do not lean on the exact numbers.

**About as good as a traditional warm-up, not clearly better.** Portillo et al. (2018, *Journal of Voice*, 32(2):200–208) gave 30 contemporary commercial (CCM) singers either a 15-minute physiological warm-up built on SOVTs or a traditional open-vowel warm-up. The study found **no statistically significant differences** between the two groups. That held for airflow, for fold contact, for the sound measurements, and for how the singers rated their own voices. Both warm-ups made singers feel better, and neither came out ahead on the objective measures. This is a null result, not evidence that SOVTs beat other warm-ups, and it reflects where the research stands today. The case for starting with them rests on the mechanical logic, a lower pressure threshold and less impact load on the folds, more than on head-to-head trials.

One caveat. Lip trills and tongue trills add a second vibrating surface, so the back-pressure wobbles instead of holding steady. A straw or a hum keeps the load even. Some researchers split SOVTs into "fluctuating" and "steady-state" types for that reason. Whether the split matters for a healthy singer warming up is not settled.

## Why they work well at the passaggio

Your **passaggio** ("passage") is the stretch of range where your voice hands off between chest-dominant and head-dominant singing. It is the place where notes tend to flip, crack, or thin out. In contemporary styles the handoff usually sits around E4–F#4 for men and A4–B4 for women. Treat those as rough markers, because the exact pitch and the feel of it vary a lot by person, voice weight, and style. Classical teaching puts the zone slightly differently and names a second passaggio higher in the range.

The major method schools (Speech Level Singing, Estill, Complete Vocal Technique) agree on one point here. The break is a coordination challenge, not a fixed piece of anatomy. They still frame the transition differently and address it in different ways.

SOVTs help at the break for a mechanical reason. The same back-pressure that lowers threshold pressure also steadies the folds while they shift between registers. Your lips or the straw carry part of the acoustic load, so the folds are less likely to flip coordination all at once. Many singers can glide through an old hard break on a lip trill long before they can do it on an open vowel. That gap is a useful signal. It points at coordination, not at the ceiling of your range.

## The main SOVT options and how they differ

**Lip trill ("brr").** Your lips flutter while you make sound. The jaw and tongue stay out of the way, and those two are common sources of the tension that interferes with vocal coordination. If the trill keeps cutting out on the way up, it is finding that interference for you. You are not doing it wrong.

**Straw phonation.** You make sound through a narrow straw, typically 2–5 mm across. It gives you steady back-pressure with no lip flutter to keep going. That makes it a good option for singers who cannot sustain a lip trill. Voice clinics lean on it because the straw's width and length are easy to control.

**Humming.** Close your lips for /m/, or rest your tongue tip at your upper teeth for /n/. This is the gentlest of the four, which makes it a good check-in or entry point. It also builds less back-pressure than a straw or a lip trill.

**Tongue trill ("rr").** Your tongue tip trills against the hard palate, the roof of your mouth. It works like a lip trill mechanically, but it is a different physical skill. Plenty of singers can do one and not the other.

No strong evidence says one type beats the others for a healthy singer warming up. Use whichever one you can sustain consistently.

## Common mistakes

**Pushing through the trill.** When a lip trill stops, especially as you go higher, the usual cause is too much air pressure or too much muscular effort. Ease off and it normally restarts. Push harder and you reinforce the exact tension you were trying to release.

**Warming up with nothing else.** SOVTs lower the pressure you need to make sound and calm the outer neck muscles. They do not replace open vowels, register coordination, or agility work. Titze recommended doing SOVTs first, then glides, then more demanding tasks. The clinical reasoning behind that order is sound. You get a lower threshold pressure and less impact load on the folds. No trial has shown the order produces better outcomes than another one in healthy singers. Lip-trilling and then jumping straight to belt-level intensity skips steps most practitioners consider important.

**Skipping the cool-down.** The same exercises that warm you up can close a session. After heavy singing, 60 seconds of descending lip trills or straw work helps release mechanical load from the folds.

## Try it: Rossini Lip Trill

The **Rossini Lip Trill** embedded below is a full-range SOVT warm-up. It climbs and descends an arc of an octave and a half (scale degrees 1–3–5–8–10–12 and back down). The piano doubles the melody, so you always have a clear pitch to match. That matters, because a lip trill makes it harder to hear your own pitch than an open vowel does. The exercise runs as follow-along and is not scored: the lip buzz reliably confuses microphone-based pitch detection, so we leave detection off. Match the piano, keep the trill fluid through the middle of your range, and notice where it wants to stop. That spot is usually your passaggio.

It steps through keys a half step at a time, up and then back down through your voice part's range. Covering your whole working range takes a few minutes.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are SOVT exercises safe for a sore voice?**
Voice clinicians often use SOVT exercises with patients recovering from voice disorders, precisely because they take less effort. Less effort is not the same as no risk. Singing on a damaged or acutely inflamed voice box still means vibration and mechanical impact. If you are hoarse from ordinary fatigue or mild overuse, gentle humming or a straw is reasonable. Other hoarseness needs a professional look first. If it comes from illness, from significant overuse, or from any cause that has lasted more than four weeks, see a laryngologist for evaluation before you continue voice exercise (AAO-HNS 2018 clinical guideline).

**How long should I do SOVT exercises in a warm-up?**
Most practitioners recommend three to five minutes at the start of a warm-up, then more demanding work after that. Longer sessions, such as the 20-minute protocol in the Savareh 2021 study, sit closer to a therapy dose than to a singer's normal warm-up. More is not automatically better. The job is to bring the threshold pressure down and let the outer neck muscles settle, then build on that foundation.

**Can I replace my whole warm-up with lip trills?**
Lip trills are an excellent starting point, but a complete warm-up covers more ground. You also want register work (glides across your range), agility (staccato and scale patterns), and vowel work (open sounds on scales). Using only SOVTs leaves that second half of the sequence untrained.

**Why does my lip trill keep stopping?**
Most often it is too much air pressure. Send less air and see whether the trill steadies. Sometimes it is tension in the lips or jaw instead. Support your cheeks gently with two fingers and see whether the trill lasts longer. If cheek support helps a lot, some facial tension is interfering.

**Do lip trills help you sing higher?**
Not directly. They do not add notes to the top of your range. What they do is make the handoff between registers easier inside the range you already have. Notes that were physically available, but blocked by coordination or tension, become reachable. Adding notes at the top takes consistent head voice and mix work over time.

**Is straw phonation better than lip trills?**
Different, not categorically better. A straw gives you steadier, more controllable back-pressure. A lip trill adds the fluctuating element of the lips themselves vibrating. Some research groups them separately for that reason, but no large-scale study on healthy singers has shown that one produces meaningfully better warm-up results than the other. Use the one you can sustain consistently.
