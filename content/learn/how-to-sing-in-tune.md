# How to Sing in Tune

Most pitch problems have a physical cause, not a talent problem. This article is for contemporary commercial music (CCM) singers: pop, rock, R&B, musical theater. It is not classical or operatic technique, though the physical machinery is the same. Maybe you go flat on long notes. Maybe you drift sharp as you climb. Or you sound great in your head and out of tune on the recording. Voice science has good explanations for all three. Below is what causes them, where teachers disagree, and what you can do about each one.

## Why Singers Go Flat

The most common reason a singer drifts flat is **collapsing breath support**, not a bad ear.

Here is the chain. Your ribs cave inward early in the phrase. The air pressure under your vocal folds (the two small flaps in your throat that vibrate to make sound) drops, and the pitch drops with it. Voice scientists call that pressure *subglottal pressure*. Sundberg's research on singing acoustics documented that it has to keep climbing as the note climbs, or the note will not stay in tune. Later acoustic research found the same. So when the pressure sags on a long note or at the end of a phrase, the note sags too.

The classical fix is to keep the ribcage open, as if you were still breathing in, even while you are singing out. Classical teachers call this *appoggio*, Italian for "lean." Many contemporary coaches say the same thing in different words: "keep the ribs buoyant," or "lean out while you sing." The wording changes from method to method. The physical goal does not.

Other common causes of flatness:

- **Air leaking through.** Your cords are not closing all the way, so air escapes instead of building pressure. The note goes soft and wobbly. A buzzy "ng" or a bratty "nay" exercise often firms it up.
- **A sagging soft palate.** The soft palate is the soft roof at the back of your mouth. When it drops, sound gets routed into your nose, which adds dips in the sound (anti-resonances) that can pull the pitch flat. Think "inner yawn," or silently say "ng" before a vowel, to feel it lift.
- **Fatigue.** Tired folds cannot hold steady closure or keep stretching. Short sessions of 15–20 minutes, repeated more often, work better than one marathon, and they cause less pitch drift from tiredness.

## Why Singers Go Sharp

Sharp pitch tends to come from the opposite problem: **too much pressure or muscular tension**.

Over-blowing means pushing more air than the note needs. That stiffens the cords past the tension the note asks for, and the note overshoots. You get the same result from clenching your jaw, gripping with the back of your tongue, or hoisting your voice box (larynx) upward to "reach" a high note. Each of those squeezes from the outside. The cords need a fine balance to vibrate at one exact pitch, and outside squeeze throws it off.

Belters who push straight through a phrase, with no lightening at the top, often land sharp. Then the cords give up and the note cracks flat. "Add ring before you add volume" is one of the points where Estill Voice Training, Complete Vocal Technique (CVT), and The Vocalist Studio (TVS) actually agree. Narrowing a small space just above the cords, called aryepiglottic narrowing or twang, gives you carrying power without forcing the air pressure underneath any higher.

## The Ear Problem: Hearing Before You Sing

Breath and tension are physical causes. There is a mental side to pitch accuracy too.

Music learning researcher Edwin Gordon called it *audiation*: hearing and processing a pitch in your head before you sing it. Research here is active. A 2024 study by Reed, Pearce, and McPherson found that singers who could imagine sound more vividly held pitch better when their hearing was disrupted. They were less easily thrown off, because they had a clearer target in their head to aim at.

A 2016 study by Bottalico, Graetzer, and Hunter in the *Journal of Voice* put numbers on the training gap. Pitch error is measured in cents, and 100 cents is one piano key. Professional singers were off by 25 cents on average. Non-professionals were off by 34.5 cents. Stranger still, the non-professionals sang *more* accurately with hearing protectors on (33.1 cents) than under normal hearing (35.3 cents). The authors called that counterintuitive. One reading is that cutting the outside noise let the singers listen to themselves from the inside, though the paper does not pin down a single cause.

**Hear the note before you sing it.** Land on the pitch in your head for one beat, then sing. This sounds like a small thing. It measurably cleans up entrances, especially on big jumps.

Interval ear training, meaning call-and-response with a piano or an app, builds that skill faster than singing songs does. Songs let you coast on your memory of the tune instead of hearing each pitch on its own.

## The Passaggio Problem: In Tune in One Register, Off in Another

Your *passaggio* is the gear-change zone between chest voice and head voice. The word is Italian for "passage," and most singers just call it the break. In classical teaching, a tenor's sits roughly around E4–G4, and a baritone's a little lower (Bb3–E4). Those are classical voice labels, and CCM singers do not always map neatly onto them. For female CCM singers the main break is usually around A4–Bb4. Individual variation is wide either way, and your voice type shifts the exact notes. This is the stretch where the muscles and the resonance of chest-dominant singing hand off to head-dominant singing.

Most singers are steadier in one of the two than in the other. Drag too much chest weight up over the break and you go sharp, then crack. Flip into a thin, underpowered head voice and you land flat. Classical teachers and most CCM methods (SLS, CVT, Estill, Somatic Voicework) agree on the goal: a gradual blend, usually called "mix," rather than an abrupt switch. Where they disagree is *how* to train it. Some start with vowel modification, narrowing the vowel as you climb. Others use lip trills or singing through a straw, exercises where the front of the mouth is partly closed (SOVT, short for semi-occluded vocal tract), to train the blend more automatically.

That disagreement is genuine. A classical-leaning teacher might have you modify EE→IH and AH→UH. A speech-level teacher may want the vowel kept intact while you adjust the shape inside your throat. Neither is wrong. They are two different handles on the same problem, and which one clicks depends on the singer.

## Why SOVT Exercises Help with Pitch

These are the exercises where you sing through a small opening: lip trills, humming, singing through a straw, and "ng" hums. Ingo Titze's 2006 paper in the *Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research* gave the physical rationale. Closing the lips partway, or singing into a narrow tube, raises **vocal tract inertance**, an acoustic property of the column of air in your throat and mouth. More inertance lowers the pressure it takes to get the folds vibrating and keep them going (the phonation threshold). So you can hold a note with less push from below. Pressure above the cords rises as a byproduct too, but Titze names inertance as the primary mechanism.

For pitch, the payoff is that these exercises train an efficient breath-to-tone trade at low effort. Because less forced air is needed, you can build the coordination for high notes without the over-blowing that pulls pitch sharp. Lip trills and straw work across the passaggio are useful for a blunt reason. They will not let you shout your way through the transition. The narrow opening regulates the pressure for you.

## How to Practice: Practical Approaches

No single drill fixes every pitch problem. A few tools show up across most methods, and have some evidence behind them:

**Drone practice.** Hold the tonic note on a keyboard or a drone app and sing scales over the top of it. When you drift off pitch, the two notes beat against each other, and you hear it the instant it happens. That tells you more than a tuner reading after the fact.

**SOVT warm-up.** Three to five minutes of lip trills or "ng" hums across your range, before anything else. This makes the folds easier to start, warms them with less impact, and settles your breath-to-tone balance. Few recommendations in CCM teaching are this consistent across methods.

**Record and compare.** What you hear from inside your head is not what comes out. A phone voice memo, played back right after the phrase, is more diagnostic than listening live.

**Slow down.** The 2016 Bottalico et al. study found that slower tempos improved pitch accuracy for non-professionals. Speed hides errors. Work the phrase slowly, see its real pitch shape, then put the tempo back.

## Try It: Nay 1-3-5-3-1

The **Nay 1-3-5-3-1** is a good one to build into your daily warm-up. It is a CCM staple, used in Speech-Level Singing, Somatic Voicework, and other contemporary methods. You sing five notes, scale degrees 1, 3, 5, 3, 1 (do, mi, sol, mi, do), on the syllable "nay," kept forward and slightly bright.

The "nay" sound (/næ/) does two jobs at once. The /n/ brings your cords together before the vowel arrives, so the note starts clean. The bright "ay" adds twang, that narrowing high in the throat that gives ring without extra air pressure. And because the shape climbs and returns rather than leaping, it walks you through the break in small steps, which makes the blend easier to keep even.

At 112 bpm in quarter notes, each note lasts long enough to settle into. The pattern steps up a half step every repetition, so you work through your range in order. If one key goes sharp or wobbly, that is useful information. Note where the drift starts. It usually marks the spot where a breath fix, or a chest-to-head blend fix, is needed.

## FAQ

### Is singing out of tune a sign I have no natural talent?

No. Research consistently shows that pitch inaccuracy drops with training for most singers. The 2016 Bottalico et al. study did find a meaningful gap between professional and non-professional singers, but the non-professionals in it improved with feedback. Audiation, the skill of hearing a pitch clearly in your head, is trainable rather than fixed at birth. Most persistent pitch problems trace back to a physical or perceptual habit you can name and change, not to an innate limit.

### Why do I sound in tune when I sing but sharp or flat on a recording?

While you sing, the sound reaches you through your skull as well as through the air. That path through the bone favors the lower frequencies, acting somewhat like a low-pass filter, so your voice sounds richer and fuller to you than it does to anyone else. A recording gives you only the air path, which is what everyone else hears. That gap can make singers misjudge their own intonation. How much of the pitch error it accounts for is not fully resolved in the research. Recording yourself regularly is still one of the most reliable ways to close the gap between self-perception and external reality.

### Does singing in tune get harder as I go higher?

For most untrained singers, yes. High notes need more air pressure under the cords, a more precise stretch of the vocal folds (the cricothyroid muscles do that job), and, around the break, a shift in the chest-to-head balance. The 2016 Bottalico et al. research found that a higher tessitura, meaning the pitch range a passage mostly sits in, was more challenging for non-professionals. The good news is that all of those are trainable. None of them is a fixed ceiling.

### Does a bad ear cause flat singing, or does flat singing cause a bad ear?

More the former than the latter. How accurately you hear small pitch differences predicts how accurately you sing them. Edwin Gordon's audiation research suggests internal hearing is the lead variable: you cannot consistently reproduce a pitch you cannot yet imagine accurately. Interval training and drone practice develop both at the same time.

### What's the difference between flat and breathy? Are they related?

Often yes. When the folds do not close firmly enough, air escapes, and that is the breathy sound. The same leak destabilizes the pressure that drives pitch, so the note goes flat and wavers. Exercises that encourage the cords to come together, such as voiced fricatives like "vvv," starting a note from vocal fry, or a bratty "nay," often improve tone quality and intonation at the same time.

### When should I see a doctor about voice problems?

If hoarseness or a change in your voice does not improve within **four weeks**, see a laryngologist (an ENT who specializes in the voice) for a scope evaluation. The 2018 AAO-HNS Clinical Practice Guideline shortened the recommended wait from 90 days to four weeks. It notes that a voice problem that persists (dysphonia) can indicate conditions including vocal fold lesions or, in some cases, cancer. See a doctor sooner if you have a neck mass, breathing difficulty, recent throat surgery, or a history of tobacco use.
