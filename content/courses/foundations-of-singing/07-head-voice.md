Head voice is the coordination that lets contemporary singers reach high notes without shouting, and without flipping into the thin, airy sound most people call falsetto. Where exactly head voice ends and falsetto begins is one of the places teaching methods disagree, and the guide lays out the camps. For this lesson, the working definition is simple: high, light, and still connected to the rest of your voice.

The reason this lesson comes after chest voice is that most singers try to get to high notes by dragging chest voice upward until it breaks. This lesson practises the opposite. You start high and come down.

## What to notice

The main exercise is "vwohm." Two short downward slides, from the sixth of the scale to the fourth, and from the fourth to the tonic, with a rest between them. The "vw" start is gentle by design, a small semi-occlusion like a tiny lip trill, so the note starts without a push. Each key steps up a half step, so over the run of the exercise head voice is led upward gradually rather than grabbed at.

Listen for the start of each slide. If it begins with a click or a little burst, you are pressing the onset. If it begins airy and the tone only arrives partway through, you have slipped toward falsetto. The good version starts clear and quiet at the same time.

The second exercise is the "ng" siren, a continuous slide up and down on the sound at the end of "sing." It is scored, but treat the score loosely; sirens are about smoothness. If the siren has a bump or a flip in it somewhere, that spot is your passaggio, and the next lesson is about it.

## What done means

Sing "vwohm" through, log it, and get through at least one siren without a flip. Compare the top keys of "vwohm" against the "nay" exercise from lesson five. Many people find the same notes easier when they approach them from above.

Add "vwohm" to your routine if high notes are your goal. The siren is a good warm-up addition after the trill, since it covers the same ground with the mouth open.
