This is the first lesson with scored pitch work, and it comes after warm-ups on purpose. Pitch problems mostly have physical causes, and the two most common ones, breath that collapses on long notes and a register shift that pulls the pitch off, are both easier to hear once the voice is warm.

The guide behind this lesson goes through why singers go flat, why they go sharp, and what to do about each. This lesson is about the two exercises and, just as much, about reading what the app tells you afterward, because the score alone is not the useful part.

## What to notice

The first exercise is "nay" on a 1-3-5-3-1 pattern. The vowel is deliberately bright and a little nasal, the kind of sound you might make imitating a cartoon. That brightness helps the folds close cleanly, which is one reason the exercise is a staple in contemporary and musical-theatre teaching. Do not soften it into a polite "neh."

The second is a descending 5-to-1, also on "nay." Starting on the top note and stepping down is harder than it sounds, because the tendency is to reach for the low note and go flat on the way. Think of each note as something you settle onto from above.

After each key, open the per-note breakdown. Look at the sign, not just the size. A row of small minus numbers means you are consistently under, which usually points at breath or at a vowel that has gone dull. One note that is far off while the rest are close usually points at the register shift, and the app's Coaching tab will pick that note out for you and let you replay it against the correct version.

## What done means

Sing both exercises through in Standard mode, log them, and look at the breakdown for at least one key of each. Compare the five-note scale from lesson one if you logged it; the Progress tab shows both.

For your routine, the "nay" 1-3-5-3-1 is the better daily pick of the two. Add the descending one too if the breakdown showed you going flat on the way down.
